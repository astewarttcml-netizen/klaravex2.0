A phishing email is a message designed to trick you into doing something you would not normally do — clicking a link, entering your password on a fake website, paying an invoice that isn't real, or installing software that hands control of your computer to an attacker. They are responsible for the vast majority of small-business cyber incidents and most consumer identity theft.

This guide teaches you to recognize phishing on sight, before you click. With practice, you can spot most attempts in under five seconds.

## The five tells of a phishing email

Every well-built phishing email tries to hit at least one of these buttons. Learn them and most phish bounces off you.

### 1. Urgency or fear
"Your account will be closed in 24 hours." "Unusual activity detected." "Action required immediately." Real companies do not write like this. They give you weeks to act, not hours.

### 2. The sender's email address doesn't match the brand
A legitimate email from Microsoft will come from a microsoft.com address. From Chase, chase.com. From PayPal, paypal.com.

A phishing email pretending to be Microsoft might come from:
- `microsoft-security@gmail.com`
- `support@microsft.com` (note the missing "o" — easy to miss)
- `noreply@microsoft.account-update.com` (the real domain is account-update.com, not microsoft.com)
- `Microsoft Security Team <random-address-here@scammer-site.tk>`

Always check the actual address, not the display name. On most email apps you can tap or hover over the sender's name to see the real address.

### 3. The link doesn't go where it says it goes
A link in an email can SHOW one address and POINT to a different one. Before you click any link, hover your cursor over it (on a computer) or long-press the link (on a phone). The real destination shows up in a small popup. If the popup shows something different from what the link text says — for example, the link says "chase.com" but the popup shows "chse-secure-login.ru" — do not click.

### 4. Generic greeting
Real companies you do business with know your name. A phishing email often opens with "Dear Customer," "Dear User," "Dear Valued Member," or just "Hello." Anyone can send to a million addresses with the same greeting.

### 5. Asks for something a real company would never ask for
Real banks, the IRS, Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, and the social security administration will never:

- Ask you to confirm your password by email.
- Ask you to send your social security number or full credit card number by email.
- Ask you to download and install something to "fix" your account.
- Threaten arrest unless you pay by gift card.
- Ask you to forward your two-factor authentication code to anyone.

If a message asks for any of these — even one — it is a phish. Stop reading and delete.

## What to do when you see a suspicious email

1. **Do not click any link.** Not even out of curiosity.
2. **Do not reply.** Replying confirms your address is active and you'll get more.
3. **Do not download any attachment.** Especially anything ending in `.zip`, `.exe`, `.iso`, `.docm`, `.htm`, `.html`, or `.js`.
4. **If you're not sure** whether it's legitimate, go to the company's website directly by typing the address into your browser (do not use the link in the email). Log in there. If there's a real problem with your account, the website will tell you.
5. **Report and delete.** Most email apps have a "Report phishing" button. In Gmail it is on the three-dot menu; in Outlook it is on the ribbon. Reporting helps the email provider block similar messages to other people.

## What to do if you already clicked

It happens. Don't panic, but move quickly.

1. **If you entered a password** on a fake site: change that password on the real site right now, and on any other site where you used the same password. Turn on two-factor authentication while you're there.
2. **If you downloaded a file:** disconnect the computer from the internet (turn off WiFi or unplug the Ethernet cable). Run your antivirus. If anything was opened, get professional help — call Klaravex at +1 (424) 348-6010 or any IT support you trust.
3. **If you replied with information:** assume that information is now in someone else's hands. Change passwords on the affected accounts; if you shared a credit card number, call the card issuer and freeze the card.
4. **If you sent money** by wire, gift card, or crypto: call your bank immediately. The first hour matters; recovery is much less likely after that.

## Special cases small businesses should know

### "Invoice from your CEO/Founder"
A common scam: an email arrives that looks like it's from your boss, asking you to wire money or buy gift cards "for a client gift, send me the codes." The boss is not the sender — the address is forged or close-looking. Pick up the phone and call your boss directly to confirm. Never act on a money-moving email without a voice confirmation.

### "Microsoft 365 password expired"
Often: "Your password expired. Click here to keep using your account." Real Microsoft password-expiration messages happen INSIDE the Microsoft account portal, not in your inbox. Go to office.com directly and log in. If a real password reset is needed, the page will tell you.

### "Mercury / Chase / your bank fraud alert"
Banks do send alerts, but they never ask you to "confirm your password by clicking a link." If you see a suspicious transaction in an alert, log in to the bank directly via the app on your phone — not the link in the email.

### "DocuSign / SharePoint / Dropbox shared with you"
If you weren't expecting a shared document, don't open it. Call or message the supposed sender on a different channel to ask, "Did you actually share this with me?"
