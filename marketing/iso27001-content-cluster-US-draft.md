### US firms are being asked for ISO 27001 — usually by a client who won't sign without it.

For years, ISO 27001 felt like a European concern. That's changed. US firms now run into it when an enterprise customer, an international partner, or an EU-based client makes certification a condition of the contract. SOC 2 answers the US market; ISO 27001 is what unlocks the rest of the world. If a deal is stalling on a security questionnaire that mentions ISO 27001, this is why.

Here's what ISO 27001 actually is, how it relates to the frameworks you already use, and what "ready" looks like for a firm without a security team.

### What ISO 27001 is (in plain terms)

ISO 27001 is an international standard for an **Information Security Management System (ISMS)** — a documented, risk-based program for protecting information, independently certified by an accredited auditor. Unlike a one-time checklist, it's a system you operate and prove over time. The current version is ISO 27001:2022, whose Annex A organizes controls into four themes — organizational, people, physical, and technological.

Certification runs on a multi-year cycle: an initial two-stage audit, then annual surveillance audits, with recertification every three years. That cadence is why "readiness" matters — you're building something you'll sustain, not cramming for one exam.

### How it relates to what you already have (SOC 2, NIST CSF)

If you've done SOC 2 or mapped to NIST CSF, you're not starting over. The frameworks overlap heavily on access control, encryption, risk assessment, vendor management, and incident response. The differences that matter:

- **SOC 2** is a US attestation report by a CPA firm against the Trust Services Criteria; **ISO 27001** is an international certification by an accredited body against a defined ISMS.
- **NIST CSF** is a voluntary framework, not a certification; it's an excellent backbone that maps cleanly onto ISO 27001's controls.
- A firm that's SOC 2-ready is often **60–80% of the way** to ISO 27001 readiness — the gap is the ISMS structure (scope, risk treatment, Statement of Applicability, management review) rather than the controls themselves.

### What "ready" looks like

A defined ISMS scope; a documented risk assessment and risk treatment plan; a Statement of Applicability; assigned security roles; access control with MFA and periodic reviews; encryption of sensitive data; an asset inventory with classification; documented incident and continuity processes; and evidence that you actually review and improve the program. A firm with these can walk into a Stage 1 audit without a fire drill.
