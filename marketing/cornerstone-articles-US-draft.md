# Article 1 — The FTC Safeguards Rule for Accounting & Tax Firms: What's Required, and How to Get Ready
**Suggested URL:** `klaravex.com/resources/ftc-safeguards-rule-accounting-firms`
**Status of the rule:** IN EFFECT. The information security program requirements have applied since June 2023; the breach-notification requirement has applied since May 2024.
**Target reader:** Principal / partner / office manager at a CPA, tax, or bookkeeping firm.

---

If your firm prepares tax returns or handles client financial data, the Federal Trade Commission considers you a "financial institution." That isn't a metaphor. Under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, tax preparation and accounting are financial activities, and the moment you collect a client's Social Security number, income records, or bank details, you fall under the **FTC Safeguards Rule** — the same data-protection standard that applies to lenders and brokers.

Most small firms don't know this until a client asks, a peer gets breached, or a notice arrives. Here's what the rule actually requires, and what a prepared firm looks like.

## What the rule requires

The Safeguards Rule requires you to build, document, and maintain a written information security program. The core elements include:

- **A Qualified Individual** responsible for your security program. This can be an employee or an outside provider — a virtual CISO. Most small firms don't have a security person on payroll, which is exactly why the rule allows you to designate one externally.
- **A written risk assessment** identifying where client information lives and how it could be exposed.
- **Access controls and encryption** for customer information, at rest and in transit.
- **Multi-factor authentication** for anyone accessing systems that hold client data. Authenticator apps and hardware keys are preferred over text-message codes.
- **Vendor oversight.** Your tax software, your cloud storage, your portal — each is an exposure point you're responsible for assessing.
- **An incident response plan**, monitoring and logging, secure data disposal, staff training, and a regular written report on the program.

## The breach-notification clock

Since May 2024, the rule adds a hard deadline. If you discover a security breach involving the unencrypted information of **500 or more** clients, you must notify the FTC **within 30 days**. That's a short window to discover, scope, and report an incident — which is only realistic if you have monitoring and an incident response plan already in place. Firms that wait until a breach to think about this miss the deadline.

## What "ready" looks like

Readiness here isn't a binder. It's a working program: a named owner, MFA enforced everywhere client data is accessed, encryption on, vendors assessed, logs being watched, and a tested plan for the day something goes wrong. A firm that has these can answer an FTC inquiry — or a client's security questionnaire — with evidence, not improvisation.

# Article 2 — The Proposed 2026 HIPAA Security Rule Update: What's Coming for Small Practices, and How to Prepare
**Suggested URL:** `klaravex.com/resources/proposed-2026-hipaa-security-rule-update`
**Status of the rule:** PROPOSED. This is a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) issued by HHS/OCR in December 2024. It is **not final**, the finalization target has slipped, and it could change, be delayed, or be withdrawn. Treat it as direction, not law.
**Target reader:** Owner / office manager / compliance lead at a small medical or dental practice.

---

In December 2024, the HHS Office for Civil Rights proposed the most significant update to the HIPAA Security Rule in two decades. It is still a proposal — not a final rule — and it faces real industry pushback over cost. But the direction is unambiguous, and the practices that start preparing now will absorb the change without a scramble if and when it lands.

Here's what the proposal would change, and what a small practice can do today regardless of the final outcome.

## What the proposal would change

The current Security Rule labels many safeguards "addressable," which practices have long treated as optional. The proposed update removes much of that flexibility and would make several controls mandatory:

- **Encryption of electronic protected health information** (ePHI) at rest and in transit, no longer addressable.
- **Multi-factor authentication** for systems that access ePHI.
- **Faster incident response and reporting**, including tighter timelines.
- **Annual penetration testing and regular vulnerability scanning.**
- **Stronger business associate oversight**, including verification that your vendors actually have the safeguards they claim.
- **A more rigorous, documented risk analysis** with asset inventories and network mapping.

## Why "wait and see" is the wrong posture

It's tempting to ignore a proposed rule. Two reasons not to. First, almost everything in the proposal is already a security best practice — encryption, MFA, vendor verification, and incident response aren't speculative; they're what a defensible practice does today, and what current OCR enforcement already expects under the existing rule. Second, if the rule finalizes, the practices that prepared early will be ready, while everyone else competes for the same scarce help against a deadline.

You don't have to bet on the proposal's fate to benefit from getting ready. You're hardening the practice either way.

## What to do now

- **Run a real risk analysis.** Not a checklist — an actual inventory of where ePHI lives and how it could be exposed.
- **Turn on encryption and MFA everywhere ePHI is touched.** These are the two highest-leverage controls in the proposal and the two most common gaps we find.
- **Get your Business Associate Agreements in order**, and verify — don't assume — that your cloud vendors have the safeguards they attest to.
- **Stand up monitoring and an incident response plan** so a phishing click doesn't become a reportable breach you discover too late.
- **Name an owner.** The Security Rule already requires a designated Security Official. Make sure it's a real person with real authority — internal or outsourced.
