# KLARAVEX — YouTube Series · Episode 01
## "I Left San Francisco to Start Over in Berlin. It Was the Best Technical Decision I Ever Made."

- **Runtime:** ~5 minutes
- **Format:** Faceless / IT Guy character
- **Voice:** Dry, authoritative, specific
- **Status:** Script complete, production pending

---

PRODUCTION NOTE: All dialogue is spoken by the IT Guy character. Stage directions in blue italics are for the animator/editor only — they do not appear on screen. Pause marks [pause] indicate a deliberate beat of 1–2 seconds. The character maintains a flat, assured expression throughout. No reaction mugging.


## HOOK / PROBLEM — 0:00–0:42 · ~105 words

[VISUAL: IT Guy at a clean desk. San Francisco skyline behind him — minimal, iconic. The skyline slowly dissolves into a Berlin roofline. Fernsehturm just visible at the right edge. No fanfare. Just the swap.]

In 2022, I was making good money in San Francisco.
Senior systems engineer. Enterprise clients. A badge that opened doors.
By every metric Silicon Valley actually respects — I was doing well.

I also hadn't taken more than four consecutive days off in three years.
My commute was forty-five minutes each way through a city that was dissolving at the edges.
And every single quarter, the answer to 'how's the team doing' was 'we're scaling aggressively.'

So I did something that confused everyone who knew me in tech.
I packed two bags, told exactly three people, and moved to Berlin.

[VISUAL: Cut. Black frame for exactly one second. Then open on a Berlin apartment window, morning light.]

## CONTEXT — 0:42–1:48 · ~155 words

[VISUAL: The IT Guy is now at a different desk. Same character. Different city. A world map begins to appear behind him — quiet, minimal.]

Let me give you some context, because this channel isn't lifestyle content.
It's about IT. Specifically, the kind that actually matters inside real businesses.

Before San Francisco, I spent eight years at two institutions you've definitely heard of.
One was a bank that nearly collapsed in 2008 and didn't.
The other was an international development organization headquartered in Washington, D.C. — and I use the word 'headquartered' loosely, because I was usually somewhere else.

Sixty-three countries. Some of them had fiber networks faster than anything in the Bay Area. Some of them had a shared laptop and a diesel generator.
Both environments taught me the same thing: infrastructure isn't about equipment. It's about decisions.

When I landed in Berlin, I brought both institutions with me in my head.
I've been using them every day since.

[VISUAL: World map behind him — dots light up across continents as he speaks. Africa, Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe, Latin America. The dots don't animate dramatically. They just appear, quietly. The map fades as he finishes.]

## BODY — 1:48–3:42 · ~310 words

[VISUAL: IT Guy, Berlin desk. Clean composition. No clutter. Window light on left.]

Here's what nobody told me about Berlin — and I mean nobody. Not the expat forums, not the LinkedIn posts, not the people who'd made the move before me.

The clients here are serious.

[VISUAL: Short pause. Let that land.]

In San Francisco, I spent roughly forty percent of my time managing expectation theater. Slide decks about slide decks. Roadmaps for roadmaps. Rooms full of very confident people who did not know what they were talking about but had strong opinions about it anyway.

My first client in Berlin was a thirty-person law firm. End-of-life Cisco ASA, no network segmentation, GDPR exposure they didn't know they had.

They gave me their requirements in one meeting. Asked two technical questions. We agreed on scope, timeline, and a fixed price.

Then they left me alone to do the work.

[VISUAL: [pause — 2 seconds]]

I finished in a four-hour window on a Saturday morning. They passed their GDPR audit six weeks later. The partner sent me a single email: 'Clean report. Thank you.'

That's it. That's the entire communication chain for a job that in San Francisco would have generated forty-seven Slack messages and three retrospectives.

Now — did I miss the salary? Yes. For about three months.
Did my partner and I miss San Francisco specifically?

[VISUAL: [slight pause — let it breathe]]

Not really. Berlin had other things going for it.

[VISUAL: IT Guy gives a minimal nod. Doesn't elaborate. The network diagram on the wall behind him quietly shifts from red warning states to green operational states while he speaks. Subtle. Not called out.]

## REHOOK — 3:42–4:02 · ~42 words

[VISUAL: Slow push in on the IT Guy. Frame tightens slightly. He leans forward just barely.]

But here's the part nobody in tech talks about when they move to Europe.

The compliance environment that looks like a burden from the outside is actually the most powerful business development tool I've ever had.

[VISUAL: Hold on him. Don't cut.]

## CLIMAX — 4:02–4:42 · ~115 words

[VISUAL: Behind the IT Guy, a simple graphic: a regulation document that quietly transforms into a pipeline of incoming calls. Understated. No explosion of color.]

In the US, cybersecurity compliance is optional until it isn't. Companies ignore it until there's a breach, a lawsuit, or a regulatory action they can't bury.

In Germany, it's a baseline. And that baseline created a market.

Every company that hasn't upgraded its infrastructure in three years has a compliance deadline approaching right now. NIS2. DSGVO audit cycles. Cyber insurance requirements that tighten every quarter.

I walked into a market where the regulatory environment does half my business development for me.

The law firm called. Then their accountants called. Then their accountants' clients called.

I haven't run a single ad.

[VISUAL: The IT Guy sits back slightly. One beat.]

## CONCLUSION — 4:42–5:05 · ~80 words

[VISUAL: IT Guy turns to face straight into frame. Berlin night skyline behind him now — warm amber from street lights, the Fernsehturm unmistakable. Clean, composed shot.]

This channel covers the things I actually deal with in practice.
Microsoft 365 configurations most companies get wrong. AI implementations that work versus the ones that get sold to you. What NIS2 actually requires, in English, without the consultant markup.

If you're running a business in Germany — or thinking about it — stick around.

And if you're sitting in a San Francisco office right now, wondering if there's a version of this career that makes more sense...

There is. It's just in a different time zone.

[VISUAL: IT Guy gives a single nod. No smile, no wink, no exaggerated CTA energy. The frame holds for two seconds. Clean fade to black. klaravex.com appears center, white on black, for three seconds. Then gone.]

## DIRECTOR NOTES
- **Tone:** The IT Guy never tries to be likeable. He is simply correct. The audience earns the warmth by reading between lines. Any attempt to make him 'relatable' through mugging or vocal energy spikes will break the character entirely.
- **Pacing:** The [pause] marks are non-negotiable. The silence is doing as much work as the words. The line 'Not really. Berlin had other things going for it.' followed by silence is the most character-defining moment in the episode — do not rush past it.
- **Music:** No music bed under dialogue. Consider a single ambient low tone under the end card only. If in doubt, silence is correct.
- **The partner reference:** It is casual, unemphasized, and buried inside a longer sentence. It is not a reveal. It is texture. Treat it accordingly.
